Physics notes

Why a new metric. The Schwarzschild metric describes one body. In general relativity two bodies can't simply sit still side by side: they fall together (in a few milliseconds here) or orbit, and a real orbiting pair needs numerical relativity on a supercomputer, with no fixed "redshift from A to B" to show. This page uses the exact static two-body solution instead, and pays for it with a strut (below).

Model. The Weyl / Israel–Khan "double Schwarzschild" vacuum solution, in Weyl's coordinates (ρ = distance from the line through both stars, z = position along it, φ = angle around it):

U obeys the ordinary flat-space Laplace equation, so the two stars' U simply add, exactly. Each star is a Schwarzschild "rod" on the axis, of length 2m (m = GM/c²), centred at zi:

With one star alone this is exactly Schwarzschild, with areal radius . The second function γ follows from U by a line integral; for rods it has a closed form (pairs of rod ends a, b):

The self-tests check it against a numerical integration of γ's field equations.

The strut. On the axis between the stars γ has a constant value γ0 ≠ 0, a conical singularity: a thin line where a small circle around the axis has more than 2π times its radius. It acts like a rod pushing the stars apart, with force

(d = Weyl distance between the rod centres). Far apart, F becomes Newton's G M₁M₂/d². No real material could be this strut: it is the price of a static picture. Off the axis it has no effect on anything shown, and on the axis it changes no clock rate or redshift (those depend on U only). It does enter the ruler distance and the light time along the axis through the factor eγ0, which is shown and is small (under 1% for these stars unless they nearly touch).

Clocks and redshift. The spacetime is static, so every static observer has a well-defined clock rate compared with one far away, and light between any two static observers shifts by their ratio, whatever path it takes:

Since U = U₁ + U₂, the clock rate is the product of the two stars' factors (not their sum, as in the weak-field approximation). Between the stars U has a highest point: the reddest point. Light from star 1's surface reddens until it gets there and blues afterwards, and a static clock there runs fastest of all clocks on the line. A static observer there feels no pull either way. The colour of the beam and the clock-rate curve in the panel are the same curve: the wavelength measured at each spot is λA·eU−UA.

The wave. On the axis, light obeys , and a ruler measures . The wave keeps its phase along with . Light time A → B is L/c on the distant observer's clock. By symmetry, light on the axis travels straight along it.

The stars. Each star's outside is taken to be the field of a Schwarzschild rod of its mass. That is exact for one star alone (Birkhoff's theorem) and for two black holes (Israel–Khan). For two stars it leaves out their tidal squeezing of each other. Each star's surface is drawn where its own Schwarzschild radius equals R = 12 km, which keeps its equator's circumference at 2π × 12 km. Masses stop at 3.5 M☉, below Buchdahl's limit (R > 9/8 rs, about 3.6 M☉), which is a result for a single star. Black holes are not included in this version: in Weyl's coordinates a horizon is drawn as a line segment.

Reference frames. Each frame belongs to a static observer: far away, O, or at the camera. Compared with that observer's clock, a static clock elsewhere runs at , and A's light reaches the observer with . Playback follows the observer's clock. Lengths don't change between these frames: static observers share the same slices of space.

Visual choices (not physics)

• Positions (grid and landscape) are drawn in Weyl's coordinates (ρ, z), the coordinates the solution is written in. Far from the stars they become ordinary distances. Near a star they are squeezed: a 12 km star is drawn as a slightly elongated body about 10 km across. The readouts always give true ruler distances.

• Grid with ruler bands (the other grid style). The grid lines are straight lines of Weyl's coordinates. A 3D grid can't show curved space without distorting something, so instead of bending the lines the page marks true length on them: each line is painted in alternating bright and dim bands, each 5 km long by rulers, counted from the grid's middle planes. Where bands crowd, there is more true distance than the drawing shows. The grid is drawn at true scale.

• Anchored grid (the default grid). The one-star page lays its grid out in ruler distance along the straight-out lines from the star's centre, anchored on a sphere. Two stars have no centre and no special spheres, so here the anchor is a point on the line through both stars. From it, the straightest paths of space (geodesics) are followed in every direction: each grid point keeps its flat-grid direction from the anchor and sits at its flat-grid distance measured with rulers along that path (Riemann normal coordinates, which don't depend on any choice of coordinates). Paths from a point on the line stay in a plane through the line, so a fan of paths in one such plane, turned about the line, covers all of space. In that plane, with f = γ − U and ψ the path's heading:

The self-tests check these paths against independent calculations: in Schwarzschild coordinates for one star, and with numerically integrated potentials for two. The grid is undisturbed at the anchor itself. Away from it, true distances along the paths are longer than in flat space, so the grid is drawn pulled in toward the anchor and bent around the stars. Paths that hit a star stop, and paths passing on either side of a star spread apart (space here curves like a saddle), so the region behind each star, as seen from the anchor, is left empty: the star's shadow. With one star this is a point-anchored version of the one-star page's grid, not the same picture (that one is anchored on a whole sphere). For anchors between the stars, the anchor sits on the strut, where space has 0.2% too much angle around the line; right next to the line the grid can't match flat space exactly there. Anchors off the line (O anywhere, or the camera) need full 3D paths and aren't built yet.

• Clock-rate landscape. A graph, not an embedding: the plane through both stars, sunk by × (1 − dτ/dt). The depth shows how slowly clocks run, compared with a clock far away, which is what makes the redshift. (For the shape of space itself, see the true-length surface below.) Inside each star the exterior solution doesn't apply; the star is drawn as an orange plateau at its surface value.

• True-length surface. The plane through both stars at one moment, bent in flat 3D so that every distance along it is the true ruler distance, : the two-star version of Flamm's paraboloid. The lengths fix how this plane curves (for a static spacetime, Einstein's equations tie the curvature to the clock rate N = dτ/dt: , so the plane's curvature is , with c the radius of the circle around the line; for one star this is Flamm's −rs/2r³). But lengths don't fix how the plane bends in the made-up vertical direction, and with two stars many shapes fit them almost equally well. Two stated conventions pin it down: along the line through both stars the surface tilts so that , the clock rate compared with the fastest clock on that piece of line (a far clock beyond the stars, the reddest point's clock between them); and of all shapes with these lengths, the least bent one is taken. The second matters where space is nearly flat (far from both stars, and between them when they are far apart): a nearly flat surface can bend freely without stretching, like paper, so lengths alone would leave kilometre-sized bumps there. For one star both are exactly Flamm's paraboloid, whose tilt obeys cos(tilt) = √(1 − rs/r). The surface is computed on a mesh of about 2,000–3,000 points in prolate spheroidal coordinates (the star centres as foci, about 3 km apart next to the stars), by Levenberg–Marquardt with a banded Cholesky preconditioner for conjugate gradients (Nocedal & Wright 2006). The page shows how true its edge lengths come out (about 0.01–0.03% on average). The self-tests check it against an independent Python solve and, with one star, against Flamm's paraboloid. A convergence study (finer mesh, larger outer edge) puts its shape seen from above at about ±0.3 km for the default stars, and up to ±0.8 km when they are 300 km apart. The height means nothing physical, and each star's inside isn't modelled (a flat orange lid).

• Flamm-style grid (the default grid). The one-star page's grid, built from the true-length surface. Seen from above, Flamm's paraboloid puts each point at its areal radius r; the one-star grid then slides each grid point along the straight-out (downhill) line until its true distance from the anchor equals its drawn distance, . Here the same steps use the two-star surface: look straight down for the drawing coordinates, follow the surface's downhill lines, and turn the result about the line to fill 3D. The stars, the beam and the clocks are drawn in the same coordinates. Anchors: far away (where light reaches infinity with z = 0.01), each star's own surface, the reddest point's height, O's height, or the camera's height. With one star this reproduces the one-star page's grid (self-tests). Where downhill lines part toward one star or the other (the plane through the reddest point) the grid has a crease, and with the stars' surfaces as anchor it tears there, since neighbouring points then measure from different stars. Right next to the stars the drawing follows the mesh (about 3 km), so lines can look slightly uneven there.

• Wave size: the wave is drawn times longer than real light. Ratios between wavelengths are true.

• Speed of light slider: only slows the playback. Real light crosses the picture in under a millisecond.

• Colours come from the CIE 1931 colour-matching functions (fit by Wyman, Sloan & Shirley 2013), converted to sRGB and desaturated. Infrared and ultraviolet are drawn grey; the readout always shows the true wavelength.
