Grid matches flat space…

The warping is measured by the redshift z that light from each spot has on reaching a distant observer. Lines are invisible where space is nearly flat (z ≤ 0.01), brighten with z, and are fully visible above this value. They also fade toward the edge of the drawing. This brightness is a visual choice; colour shows z itself.

Physics notes

Model. Schwarzschild vacuum exterior of a non-rotating, uncharged, spherical mass M. Distances are in km and masses in solar masses (M☉).

t is the time kept by a static observer far away ("at infinity"), and the whole animation runs on that clock. r is the areal radius: a sphere at r has area 4πr². It is not the distance to the centre.

Warped cube grid. Curved 3D space can't be drawn in flat 3D without distorting something, so this view makes one stated choice. The cube is laid out evenly in ruler coordinates: direction is kept, and the distance from the centre is , measured with rulers from an anchor radius ra where ρ = r. Each point is then drawn at its areal radius r. Near the mass a ruler length takes up less r, so the grid lines crowd inward. Along the radius, the cells shrink by exactly . Sideways sizes on the drawn cube are true at the drawn radius. Nothing is exaggerated: this is the true scale. Lines inside the body or the horizon are not drawn. Far from the mass the extra ruler distance keeps growing slowly (like ), so no choice of anchor makes the grid match flat space everywhere. The selector picks where it does: at the surface (ra = R, or rs for a black hole): the inner grid is undisturbed and the extra distance builds up outward, so distant lines sit a little inside their flat positions. Far away (ra = where the lines have faded, z = 0.01): the outer grid is undisturbed and all the extra distance is taken up on the way in, so the lines are pulled toward the mass. Around a black hole that pull is larger than the horizon itself, so the grid's centre lands on a sphere outside the horizon and the inner lines bunch up there. At the observer (ra = rO, the reference frame's observer; in the far-away frame this is the same as far away): the grid sits at its flat-space positions around the observer, and the offset grows in both directions away from them. In the camera frame it follows the camera. At the camera (ra = the camera's distance from the centre, or from the axis on Flamm's paraboloid): the same, but always following the camera, whichever reference frame is chosen. All three show the same local squeeze; only this overall offset differs. To reach far out, the grid is nested: each time the distance doubles, the line spacing doubles, and lines that belong only to a finer level fade out where the coarser one takes over.

Flamm line in the grid view (optional). The profile of Flamm's paraboloid, drawn in the vertical plane through the grid's x axis, at the same areal radius r as the grid. Each white marker hangs below a grid vertex on that axis. The length along the curve between two markers equals the grid's spacing in ρ, because both are ruler distance. The curve keeps its length by bending into the extra (meaningless) vertical direction. The grid keeps its length by packing the vertices closer together in r.

Flamm's paraboloid. The equatorial plane at one moment, embedded in flat 3D so that distances measured along the surface equal real ruler distances:

The vertical direction has no physical meaning. It's an extra dimension added only to draw the curvature, and it shows the curvature of space only. Most of everyday gravity comes from the slowing of clocks, which this picture can't show. That is why the redshift colours are there. True scale, no exaggeration. Inside a star, the surface continues as a spherical cap of radius (orange). That cap assumes uniform density (Schwarzschild's interior solution), and it meets the outside surface with the same slope.

The central mass. The body has a fixed radius R = 12 km, about the size of a neutron star. No static star can be smaller than (Buchdahl's limit), so above about 3.6 M☉ the page draws a black hole instead, with its horizon at r = rs. Real neutron stars collapse at a lower mass, which depends on nuclear physics that isn't yet known. Around a black hole, A and B can't go closer than 1.01 rs. Static observers can't exist at or inside the horizon.

Visual choices (not physics)

• Wave size: the wave is drawn times longer than real light, so you can see it. The ratios between wavelengths are true.

• Line brightness (grid and paraboloid): lines fade in with the redshift z of their location: invisible at z ≤ 0.01, fully visible at the z you choose with the slider (log scale in between). Nothing else limits how far they reach: grid and surface are drawn out to where z falls to 0.01. Their positions are unchanged. The fading only marks where space is nearly flat.

• Speed of light slider: this only slows the playback. Real light crosses the whole picture in about 0.3 milliseconds. Changing c itself wouldn't add anything new. In this geometry M and c only appear together as rs = 2GM/c², so halving c acts exactly like making the mass four times bigger. Use the mass slider for that.

• Colours come from the CIE 1931 colour-matching functions (analytic fit by Wyman, Sloan & Shirley 2013), converted to sRGB and desaturated to fit the screen. Wavelengths beyond 780 nm (infrared) or below 380 nm (ultraviolet) are invisible and are drawn grey. The readout always shows the true wavelength.

Reference frames. Each frame belongs to a static observer, one hovering at a fixed radius rO (far away, at O, or at the camera). Compared with that observer's clock, a static clock at r runs at

and light from A arrives at the observer with , a blueshift if A is farther out than the observer. Playback follows the observer's clock: per second of its time, the far clock's time t advances by . Each clock's hand always shows that clock's own proper time; only the comparison changes. Lengths don't change between these frames. Static observers share the same slices of "space at one moment", so they all measure the same ruler distances, and the grid, the paraboloid and the rulers are the same in every frame. Locally every observer's space looks flat, but the extra distance between two radii is the same number for all of them. (A freely falling observer slices spacetime differently, and for them it disappears; that would be a separate view.) In the camera frame, rO is the camera's distance from the centre (on Flamm's paraboloid, from its axis).

Radar distance. A static observer can measure distance with light: send a pulse straight up or down to a point, time its return on their own clock, and take c × half the round trip. For points on the observer's radial line,

Unlike ruler distance, this depends on whose clock times the pulse, so it changes with the frame. Next to the observer it agrees with the rulers ("a meter is a meter" locally). Farther in, light is slow by the observer's clock, so radar distances come out longer than rulers; farther out they come out shorter. Toward a black hole's horizon, the radar distance grows without limit. The scale shows cyan radar ticks above the line and orange ruler ticks below it, every 10 km. It is drawn along O's radial line; in the camera frame, at the camera's distance on that same line.

What observers see. Light bending decides apparent sizes; the extra ruler distance doesn't make rulers look short. From far away, a star of radius R ≥ 1.5 rs looks in radius, larger than it is (light grazing its surface bends toward you), and more than half its surface is visible. A black hole, or a star smaller than 1.5 rs, shows a shadow of radius , set by the photon sphere. A static observer at rO sees distant objects near the zenith smaller by (small-angle limit), and bluer and brighter: the outside sky crowds overhead. That compression belongs to the hovering observer; a freely falling observer at the same place sees the sky differently (aberration). The self-tests check this against light rays traced numerically, and the shadow against a numerical minimisation. Before these are cited on a public page they still need a published source.

In the grid view, which is drawn at areal radius r, an outward ruler is drawn long, so it looks shorter near the mass. A sideways ruler is drawn its full length L, because tangential distances are , exactly as in flat space. The labels give the drawn length of the first and last ruler. On Flamm's paraboloid, lengths along the surface are true, so every ruler looks the same length; the paraboloid fits the extra rulers in by sloping into its vertical direction. Like the clocks, each ruler belongs to static observers, and every static observer agrees on these lengths.
