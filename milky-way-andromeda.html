Physics notes

Why this works. The Milky Way's gravity makes clocks run slow by only about one part in a million (Φ/c² ≈ −10⁻⁶). At that strength the weak-field metric is accurate to about one part in 10¹² (Carroll 2004, ch. 7; Will 2014):

Φ is the ordinary Newtonian potential, so the two galaxies' potentials simply add: Φ = ΦMW + ΦM31. The galaxies are falling toward each other, but light takes 2.5 million years to cross while they close by only about 0.3 kpc (0.04%), so a still snapshot is a fair picture. No strut or exact two-body solution is needed.

Mass models (Patel, Besla & Sohn 2017, Table 2). Each galaxy has an NFW dark-matter halo (mass Mvir − Mdisc − Mbulge, cut off at the virial radius, scale radius Rvir/c), a Miyamoto–Nagai disc and a Hernquist bulge:

(The first term is the pull of the halo inside r, the second that of the halo between r and its edge.) Their halos are also "adiabatically contracted" by the stars; this page leaves that out. Without it the Milky Way model's circular speed at the Sun's distance is 200 km/s, below the measured ≈ 230–240 km/s, so the potential here is somewhat shallower than the real one. Both galaxies' discs are drawn in the plane containing the line between them (their real orientations are left out); this affects the potential only within about 20 kpc of each centre.

Clocks and redshift. A static clock runs at compared with one far away, and light between two static observers shifts by , whatever its path. Along the line, Φ is highest at the balance point, where the two galaxies' pulls cancel (the static counterpart of the Lagrange point L1): light is reddest there. Andromeda's own motion matters far more than gravity for its light: it approaches at 109 km/s (van der Marel et al. 2012), a Doppler blueshift of about 3.6 × 10⁻⁴, hundreds of times the gravitational shift. With the receiver moving with Andromeda, the page multiplies the two: , β = −109 km/s ÷ c.

Light time and distance. On the far-away clock, light along the line takes ; the second part is the Shapiro delay (a few years). A chain of rulers along the line measures , a fraction of a parsec more than the coordinate distance (in these isotropic coordinates).

Warped grid. As on the one-star page, each galaxy on its own stretches space along the lines straight out from it: in its own (areal) radius r, ruler distance grows by , m(r) the mass inside r (here the galaxy's spherically averaged mass). Each grid point is moved along each galaxy's radial line so that its ruler distance from the anchor equals its flat-grid distance, as on the one-star page, and in the weak field the two galaxies' moves simply add (to about one part in 10¹²). Anchors: far away (2 Mpc), at each galaxy's centre, at the observer, or at the camera. The moves are a fraction of a parsec, so they are drawn multiplied by the exaggeration factor.

Visual choices (not physics)

• Exaggeration (slider): the grid's warp, the colour change of the light, the difference in the clocks' hand speeds, and the landscape's depth are all drawn multiplied by this factor. The readouts are true.

• Clock-rate landscape: the plane of the line, sunk by 20 kpc × the exaggeration ÷ 10⁵ per part per million of |Φ|/c². A graph of the clock rate, not a picture of space.

• Galaxies are drawn as glowing discs 6 disc scale lengths in radius (about the edge of the visible disc); their halos (most of the mass) are invisible dark matter, shown as faint spheres at their virial radius if chosen.

• Wave size and playback speed are only for display.

Limits. No cosmic expansion: the Local Group is gravitationally bound, so space between the two galaxies does not expand (between galaxy clusters far apart it would dominate). Strong gravity near the central black holes (Sgr A*, M31*) is left out: it matters only within a tiny region around each.
